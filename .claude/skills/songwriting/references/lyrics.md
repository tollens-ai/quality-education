# Lyric writing: the craft and its sources

What the best writers on lyric craft agree on, as rules you can apply and check. Examples come
from episode 1 of *Software Quality Theory 101*, a pop-punk song sung by a coding agent in a
synthetic voice ([lyric sheet](../../../../episodes/01-you-never-told-me.md),
[song map](../../../../episodes/01-song-map.md)).

**House rules come first.** [LYRICS.md](../../../../LYRICS.md) records what the expert ear (Qing)
has ruled on real drafts. Her verdicts outrank everything here, and this file doesn't repeat them.
Where a rule below agrees with a house rule, or explains it, the note says **(house: …)**. Where
theory and a house rule disagree, the note says so, and the house rule wins.

## Principles
- **Clarity is the goal; everything else serves it.** Sondheim opens both volumes of his collected
  lyrics with the same credo: "Content Dictates Form / Less Is More / God Is in the Details / all in
  the service of / Clarity / without which nothing else matters." When a clever line and a clear
  line compete, the clear line wins.
- **Content dictates form.** Episode 1's verses are lists of over-built apps, so they borrow a
  list-song rhythm ("We Didn't Start the Fire"); the song is a complaint, so it's pop-punk.
- **A lyric is heard once, at speed.** Sondheim: too packed and "it's like an over-egged cake";
  "if the lyric is too sparse, it's dull". After one hearing, listeners transcribed 72% of lyrics
  across twelve genres (Condit-Schultz & Huron); a quarter of unaccompanied sung words were
  misheard (Collister & Huron).
- **Less is more, which doesn't mean fewer ideas.** This is a tension with the house rule "pack in
  as much information as possible". The house rule wins, and the two fit together: cut filler,
  recap and padding (Sondheim's "less"), but keep each line to one idea, so that a dense song is
  still easy to follow. Episode 1 gives one app per line and one tradeoff per phrase.
  (house: *Pack in as much information*, *One example per line*.)
- **God is in the details.** A specific detail does more work than a general one. Pattison: "the
  more specific you get, the more sense-bound you get, the more effective it is." "2FA on your gym
  log" teaches over-engineering faster than "too much security". (house: *Name concrete constraints,
  not categories*.)

## Prosody: form carries meaning
- **Every element supports the message.** Pattison defines prosody as "when all elements work
  together to support the central message of the song", and calls it the one rule among tools.
  Against a heartbreak song in tidy four-line AABB verses: "My response is: 'You'll get over it!'"
- **Stable structures resolve and unstable structures push forward.** Pattison's tools: an even
  number of lines feels settled and an odd number feels off balance. Lines of matched length settle;
  shortening or lengthening the last line unsettles. ABAB is stable and ABBA unstable. A phrase that
  starts after the downbeat feels less settled than one that starts on it. Use unstable shapes where
  a section must lead on to the next (verse ends, pre-chorus), and stable ones where it arrives
  (chorus, final line). Episode 1's pre-chorus is six bars and starts on the "&" of 1, and it hands
  over to the chorus on a band stop.
- **Stressed syllables go on strong beats.** Prosody at the level of the syllable; see
  [MUSIC.md](../../../../MUSIC.md). (house: *Rhythm*, including when a bent stress is fine.)
- **Leave space after the big line.** Pattison: "put your most important lines in places that have
  a little space after them." "I'm only reading your prompt" is followed by a band stop. (house:
  *The payoff phrase goes last, once*.)

## Rhyme
- **Know the rhyme types; they range from settled to open.** Pattison's scale: **perfect** (same
  vowel and following consonants: log/blog), **family** (same vowel, consonants of one phonetic
  family: mud/truck), **additive/subtractive** (a consonant added or dropped: cry/bribe,
  rhymes/time), **assonance** (vowel only: read/cheap), **consonance** (final consonant only).
  Perfect and family support "stable or resolved meaning"; assonance "unstable or unresolved".
- **Perfect rhymes for jokes and for arrival.** Sondheim: "A perfect rhyme snaps the word, and with
  it the thought, vigorously into place, rendering it easily intelligible; a near rhyme blurs it."
  Also: "Jokes work best with perfect rhymes." Webb goes further: a false rhyme offends the listener
  "on a subliminal level". Pop-punk choruses need end rhymes as strong as these (keep/cheap,
  grow/show). (house: *Pop-punk wants strong end rhymes*.)
- **Near rhymes where the section is meant to feel open.** This is where theory explains a house
  rule. The house rules allow a half rhyme in the tag, and a whole verse on one vowel family
  (floss, log, blog, clock). By Pattison's scale, assonance is right for a tag that winds into the
  next section: it feels open, as the tag should. (house: *A tag breaks the pattern*; *Rhyme on a
  vowel family*.)
- **Rhyme puts a word in the spotlight, so rhyme on the word that matters.** Sondheim: "All rhymes
  … draw attention to the rhymed word; if you don't want it to be spotlighted, you'd better not
  rhyme it." Put the idea or the joke on the rhyme, and keep structure words ("it", "the") off it.
  (house: *Hard-to-rhyme words go inside the line* is the fix when the key word won't rhyme.)
- **Rhyme depends on accent.** Tom Lehrer sang "Harvard" and "discovered" in a Northeastern elite
  accent to make them rhyme. Episode 1's verse 2 only rhymes in British English, so the voice is
  British. (house: *Rhyme on a vowel family*, the note on accent.)
- **Internal rhyme adds density without adding lines.** Rap and *Hamilton* stack rhymes within the
  line (the *Wall Street Journal* mapped *Hamilton*'s into "rhyme families"). Each rhyme marks a
  beat, which helps fast lines; a slow chorus needs only its end rhymes.

## Hook, title and power positions
- **The title goes in a power position.** Pattison: "always try to place the title in prominent
  (spotlighted) positions, where it is most likely to be noticed: title first, title last, title
  both first and last." Other power positions are the first and last lines of a section.
- **The hook is the title, usually, and it lives in the chorus.** Blume says every hit has its title
  in the chorus. Critics point to exceptions, and so does Blume, for "alternative" bands. Episode 1
  is an exception worth deciding on purpose: its title, "You Never Told Me", is paraphrased in the
  pre-chorus ("You didn't tell me…"), and the chorus hook is "Good for who? Good for what?".
- **Repeat the hook exactly.** Songs with more repetitive lyrics reached number 1 more often and
  faster in Billboard data from 1958 to 2012, and in lab tests they were processed more fluently
  (Nunes, Ordanini & Valsesia). Listeners also catch more words on the second and third hearing
  (Condit-Schultz & Huron), so a line that repeats has more chance of being understood. (house:
  *Repeat the hook; vary the second half*.)

## Point of view and voice
- **Fix the viewpoint, voice, time frame and setting, and signal every change.** Sheila Davis calls
  this getting "a fix on the VVTS". An unmarked shift confuses the listener. Episode 1 is sung by the
  agent in the first person to "you", in the present tense, until the final chorus answers its own
  question ("Good for you!"). That is a change the listener has been led to expect. (house: *Quotes
  vanish when sung*: in a sung lyric, someone else's view sounds like the singer's own unless the
  words attribute it.)
- **Second person pulls the listener in.** "You said make it GOOD" makes every viewer the one who
  gave the vague prompt, and "I can't read your mind" lands because the underdog says a plain,
  fair truth to them.

## Showing and telling
- **Verses show; the chorus tells.** Verses build evidence with concrete scenes, and the chorus
  states what they add up to. Episode 1: the verses show four apps built without a brief, and the
  chorus asks the question that would have stopped it. Pattison's object writing trains the
  showing: ten minutes, first thing in the morning, writing about one object with all seven senses
  ("sight, hearing, smell, taste, touch, organic, and kinesthetic"). Use it for any verse that
  comes out abstract.

## Comedy, patter and list songs
- **A list song escalates, and the biggest item goes last.** Order the items from plausible to
  absurd, so the final one lands the laugh. Episode 1's verse 1 runs confetti, 2FA, Kubernetes and
  then twelve subagents round the clock, and then the quota runs out.
- **The joke word goes on the rhyme, at the end of the line.** The rhyme is where the ear expects
  something to arrive. "Kubernetes for your BLOG" holds back the mismatch until the last word.
  Lehrer's "The Elements" lists 102 elements and saves its joke for the last couplet, on the rhyme:
  "the news has come to Harvard, / And there may be many others, but they haven't been discovered."
- **The hunt for a rhyme can be the joke.** Gilbert's stage directions have the Major-General
  "bothered for a rhyme" near the end of each verse, then finds one with a flourish. Use it once.
- **Patter is one syllable per note, fast, lightly accompanied.** Tongue-twisters and alliteration
  are the fun, if every word still reads. (house: *At patter speed, keep the template*.)
- **A parody keeps the original's rhythm and rhymes and swaps in a small, everyday subject.** "Weird
  Al" Yankovic's "Eat It" turns "Beat It" into a parent and a picky eater. The laugh comes from the
  mismatch between the grand form and the petty subject. Episode 1 does the same with a
  heartbreak-complaint genre and a vague prompt. (house: *Borrow a rhythm template*.)
- **Play it straight.** Comedy songs by Flight of the Conchords and Bo Burnham mostly get their
  laughs from exact, mundane detail sung dead seriously (our reading, not a sourced claim). The
  robot means every word.

## Teaching in a lyric without lecturing
- **Start from what sticks.** Schoolhouse Rock began when ad executive David McCall noticed his son
  could memorise Rolling Stones lyrics but not his multiplication tables. The song has to be a song
  people want to hear first, and a lesson second.
- **Let the concept sing.** In "I'm Just a Bill", a bill sings about its own journey to becoming a
  law. In episode 1 the coding agent sings about its own brief. When the concept has a point of
  view, the explanation becomes a story.
- **Make the hook a question the lesson answers.** "Conjunction Junction, what's your function?" and
  "Good for who? Good for what?" both teach by asking. The final chorus can answer the question.
  (house: *The last chorus can answer the hook*.)
- **State the definition once, as a chant.** Episode 1 says its thesis outright only in the
  breakdown. Every teaching line, joke included, must be true. (house: *Fact-check the jokes too*.)

## Writing for a synthetic voice
- **Open vowels on high and long notes.** Once the pitch passes a vowel's first resonance, sopranos
  retune that resonance to the pitch: louder and easier, "at some cost to intelligibility". Wagner
  set soprano text so the vowel suited the pitch (Smith & Wolfe). Put AH, AW and OH on high held
  notes, not EE or OO; for a female voice the risk starts around 500 Hz (B4). **Episode 1
  flag:** "WHO" (a closed OO) sits on the hook's E♭5. The MiniMax take proved it sings, and "what"
  beside it is open, but check it by ear in the code-built voice.
- **Consonants are where mishearings happen.** In Collister and Huron's study, listeners confused
  consonants, "especially voiced stops and nasals", and vowel errors drifted towards central vowels.
  Avoid depending on a b/d/g or m/n contrast for the meaning of a fast line.
- **Clusters slow the tongue, even a synthetic one.** "Twelve sub-" puts /lvs/ across a word
  boundary inside a 167 ms eighth note at 180 bpm. When a line slurs, look for a cluster first.
- **Spell for the generator, and caption the real spelling.** Acronyms and jargon come out wrong
  unless they're respelled. (house: *Spell tricky words as they're said*.)
- **Test for mondegreens.** Sylvia Wright coined the word in 1954 for "laid him on the green",
  which she had heard as "Lady Mondegreen". Word boundaries blur when a line is sung. (house: *Check
  for mishearings*.)

## Checkable
Rules a script can flag before anyone listens; a person judges each flag. LYRICS.md *Checking*
lists CMUdict's gaps (jargon, one-syllable stress, squashed words): keep a sung lexicon of overrides.
- **Rhyme type per pair:** look up both words in CMUdict (`pronouncing`). Compare the phones from
  the last stressed vowel onward, and label the pair perfect, family, additive/subtractive,
  assonance, consonance or none. Flag any end rhyme in a chorus that isn't perfect or family.
- **Rhyme scheme and line count per section:** label the end-rhyme classes (AABB, ABAB, ABBA),
  count the lines, and report each section as stable or unstable. Flag a stable pre-chorus and an
  unstable final chorus.
- **Stress on the beat:** score each syllable's dictionary stress against its metric position.
  `music/check/prosody.py` does this, calibrated on the expert's past verdicts.
- **Vowel openness on exposed notes:** for each note of a beat or longer, or above a set pitch (C5
  for a female voice), map the ARPAbet vowel to open (AA, AO, AH, AE, AW, AY, OW) or closed (IY,
  IH, UW, UH, ER). Flag the closed ones.
- **Syllables per second:** divide sung syllables by duration for each line, from the score's
  events. Flag lines above the song-fit limits in the write-episode skill (about 3–4 per second
  outside patter; the "Fire" verses run at 6).
- **Consonant clusters at speed:** count consecutive consonant phones across each note boundary,
  including across words. Flag three or more on notes shorter than an eighth at the song's tempo.
- **Title and hook placement:** check that the hook line appears in the first or last line of every
  chorus, and that it is identical each time.
- **Repeated words:** count the content words across the sheet, excluding the hook. Flag any used
  more than twice, and any rhyme word used twice in one section.
- **Homophones and resegmentation:** list the words whose CMU phones match another word's. Then run
  Whisper on the rendered vocal and diff it against the sheet, as MUSIC.md describes. Whisper
  guesses from context, so a pass is a floor, not proof.
- **Leaps on the right words:** list every leap of five semitones or more (`music/ep01/leaps.mjs`)
  and check each falls on a hook word.
