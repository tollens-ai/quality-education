# Melody writing: the craft and its sources

What corpus research, music psychology and songwriting teachers say about melody, rewritten as
rules you can apply and check. Each rule is short and bold, then one or two sentences, then an
example. The worked example is episode 1 of *Software Quality Theory 101*, "You Never Told Me": a
pop-punk song in E♭ major at 180 bpm for a synthetic female voice, whose hook leaps to the high
tonic ([song map](../../../../episodes/01-song-map.md), [score](../../../../music/ep01/score.mjs)).

**House rules come first.** The Melody section of [MUSIC.md](../../../../MUSIC.md)
hold what the expert ear (Qing) has ruled on real drafts, and this file doesn't repeat them. Her
central melody rule is "leaps are seasoning": keep big leaps rare so the hook's leap lands, because
"the octave tonic is a waste for 'oops' when the fifth would do". Most of what follows explains
why that works and how to measure it. Numbers are starting points unless a source is given.

## Contour and shape
- **Shape each phrase as an arch or a descent.** In European folksongs the most common phrase
  shape rises and then falls, and pairs of phrases make a bigger arch (Huron's melodic-arch
  study). Pop phrases also often peak early and fall: "GOOD for WHO-o-o" peaks on WHO and falls
  for the rest of the bar.
- **After a leap, turn back.** In melodies from many cultures a large interval tends to be
  followed by a change of direction. Von Hippel & Huron showed this is mostly regression to the
  mean: a leap usually lands near the edge of the melody's range, and from there most notes are
  back towards the middle. So the reliable rule is "return towards the centre", not "a leap
  must be filled".
- **A leap towards the middle needn't turn.** The same study reports that leaps moving away
  from the melody's median, or across it, tend to reverse, and leaps approaching it tend to
  continue. A fourth up from the bottom of the range into the middle can keep climbing by step.
- **Small intervals imply continuing; large ones imply turning.** Narmour's
  implication–realization model predicts that a small interval (up to five semitones in
  Schellenberg's simplification) sets up another similar interval in the same direction, and a
  large one (seven or more) sets up a smaller interval in the opposite direction. A melody that
  keeps meeting these expectations sounds natural; break one where the words need emphasis.
- **Leap up, step down.** Across Western composers, folk songs and the Beatles, small intervals
  are mostly descending and large ones mostly ascending (Vos & Troost). Climb to a peak by leap
  and come down by step. Episode 1's hook breaks this on purpose: it leaps up an octave to WHO
  and falls a sixth to G4, then climbs back by step (A♭–B♭). The double leap is the hook's
  identity, which is why nothing else in the song may leap like it.
- **Pop steps rock back and forth; they rarely run.** In folk, classical and hymn melodies a
  step is usually followed by another step in the same direction (in the Essen folksong corpus,
  43.1% of steps against 18.3% that reverse). In Billboard and Rolling Stone melodies the reverse
  holds: steps that turn back are slightly more likely (Chiu & Temperley). Neighbour-note motion
  (E♭–D–E♭, G–F–G) sounds like pop; five-note scale runs sound like a hymn.
- **Give a pop melody more range than a folk tune.** The average Billboard melody spans 19.4
  semitones with an average interval of 3.8, against 13.1 and 2.8 for folk songs (repeated notes
  removed; Chiu & Temperley). Episode 1 spans 17 semitones, B♭3 to E♭5, which is typical.
- **Save the top note for the climax.** The song's highest pitch belongs to its most important
  word, and it is sung in few places: the hook, one deliberate callback, and the last chord. In
  episode 1 E♭5 is heard only on the hook, the bridge's FOR, and the final held DONE. The hook
  repeats, but a repeated hook counts as one idea (the gang and the bots don't count; they're
  not the lead). A second peak note anywhere else spends it.
- **Make the last climax the longest.** The final time the top note appears, hold it longer or
  approach it by a new route. Episode 1's last DONE is held over a ♭VI–♭VII–I fanfare instead of
  dropping away like every earlier hook.
- **Give each section its own register band.** Verse, pre-chorus and chorus each sit in a band,
  and the bands overlap but climb. Episode 1: verse B♭3–B♭4, pre-chorus B♭3–G4 (up to B♭4 in
  the final one), chorus tail C4–B♭4 with the hook's E♭5 above it, bridge E♭4–C5 climbing to
  E♭5. The verse and chorus tail share a band, so the hook alone carries the lift.

## Motif and development
- **A motif is a rhythm plus a contour.** Keep both when you repeat it, and let the exact pitches
  change with the chords. Episode 1's verse lines share one rhythm, and each steps its contour a
  little higher as the gags escalate.
- **Repetition is what people recognise.** In a large song-recognition game ("Hooked!"), the
  quickest-recognised sections had more repetitive melodies built from more typical motifs, and
  the strongest single predictor was how prominent the vocal was (Van Balen et al. 2015). Repeat
  the chorus melody exactly, and keep the lead voice on top of the mix when it plays.
- **Sequence builds momentum.** Repeating a motif a step higher (or on the next chord) raises
  tension without writing new material. It suits pre-choruses, where each repetition should
  feel closer to the chorus. Three statements is the usual limit before it sounds mechanical.
- **Vary one parameter at a time.** When a line comes back changed, change its ending pitch, its
  pickup, or one interval, not its rhythm and contour together. An ending that lands on 2 or 5
  sounds like a question; the same line landing on 1 or 3 answers it.
- **Call and response splits one idea between two voices.** The lead states, a second voice or
  instrument answers in the gap. Episode 1's gang echoes "(who)-o" and "(what)-at" in the holes
  left by the lead's held hook notes, and the whoa-ohs answer every bridge line.
- **Build the hook from a common shape plus one odd interval.** Songs that most often get stuck
  in people's heads are "usually faster, with a fairly generic and easy-to-remember melody but
  with some particular intervals, such as leaps or repetitions that set them apart" (Jakubowski
  et al. 2016). The common shape is rise-then-fall, as in "Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star".
  Episode 1's hook is a plain arch whose odd interval is an octave leap onto the question word.
- **Spend the odd interval once.** The earworm finding only works if the unusual interval is
  unusual in this song. That is the research behind "leaps are seasoning" (house: MUSIC.md).
- **Put the hook where the title is.** Chorus melodies are "built around an important hook that
  often incorporates the song title" (Ewer). Put it on the first or last line of the chorus, on
  the section's highest or longest note.

## Melody against harmony
- **Stable tones end phrases; unstable tones move.** In major, 1, 3 and 5 are the stable
  degrees (Perricone's "stable tones"). 2, 4, 6 and 7 want to resolve, usually by step and
  usually down. Episode 1's verse tag hangs "did I do it WRONG" on G over Cm (3 of the key, a
  chord tone that is still not home) and lands "your quota's GONE" on E♭ over E♭: home.
- **An appoggiatura is the expressive non-chord tone.** A non-chord tone on a strong beat that
  resolves by step to a chord tone leans on the word. Give it to a word that should ache, not to
  a function word. "I'm SOME-one TOO" in the tag could use one.
- **Approach the target note from a step away.** A scoop or a lower neighbour a semitone or tone
  below makes a high note sound reached for rather than switched on. Episode 1's hook scoops D5
  to E♭5, and the ROGUE line scoops A♭4 to B♭4.
- **Anticipations push a stressed syllable an eighth early.** In a rock corpus the typical
  syncopation shifts an accented syllable to just before the strong beat, and the shift seems to
  work best at "perhaps around a quarter of a second" (Tan, Lustig & Temperley). At 180 bpm an
  eighth is 0.17 s, so eighth-note pushes read as syncopation; sixteenth pushes (0.08 s) sound
  like mistakes.
- **Let verses float on the pentatonic; lock choruses to the chords.** Temperley finds rock
  verses often use a "stratified" organisation: the melody moves freely on a pentatonic scale
  while the chords change underneath, and non-chord tones need not resolve. The chorus shifts to
  a "unified" organisation where the melody follows the harmony ([harmony.md](harmony.md)). So audit a
  verse for scale membership and phrase endings on 1, 3 or 5, and a chorus for chord tones.
- **Treat chord–melody tension as a dial.** Hooktheory's SongMetrics measures how often melody
  notes fall outside the current chord. The songs that score high include "Smells Like Teen
  Spirit" and "Rolling in the Deep", so tension is a style choice. Pick a level for each
  section and hold it there on purpose.

## Tension and release across sections
- **Decide whether each section is stable or unstable.** Pattison's test for prosody asks of
  every idea, "is that idea stable or unstable?", and asks the melody to match. A stable line
  ends on the tonic on a strong beat; an unstable one ends on 2 or 5, off the beat, or on a
  suspended chord. Episode 1's pre-chorus ends on F (2 of the key) over B♭, the band stopped:
  a question the chorus answers.
- **Verses talk.** Verse melodies "wander", sit lower, and use "more syncopation, quicker rhythms"
  than choruses (Ewer). Many repeated notes and a narrow band suit a list or a story. Episode 1's
  verse list lines are straight eighths within B♭3–A♭4, 70% steps.
- **Pre-choruses hold something back.** Keep the pre-chorus below the chorus peak and tighten
  it: shorter phrases, rising sequence, or the melody held on one note while the chords climb.
  Episode 1's pre-chorus is 92% steps and sinks to B♭3 on WANT, so the hook's octave leap has
  the whole range to cross.
- **Choruses sing.** Chorus melodies sit higher, use simpler rhythms "locked in to the basic beat",
  repeat more, and hold longer notes (Ewer; corpus support in MUSIC.md). Fewer syllables per bar
  than the verse is a good first check.
- **The bridge finds a new register or a new route.** Either a new band the song hasn't used, or
  a fresh approach to the old peak. Episode 1's bridge climbs by step through B♭4 and C5, then
  reaches the hook's E♭5 on FOR, its only peak outside the chorus.
- **Dense, simple melodies are current.** Across US chart melodies from 1950 to 2023, melodies
  have become less complex and denser in notes, especially since 2000 (Scientific Reports 2024).
  Patter over a few pitches is idiomatic, not lazy.

## Singability
- **Phrases end where the singer breathes.** Perricone defines a phrase partly by "the natural
  areas to breathe" and asks: "Does the vocalist have time to breathe between phrases?" At 180
  bpm four bars last 5.3 s; plan a breath at least every four bars and always before a held high
  note.
- **Don't change range too quickly.** Perricone's third test: "Does the range within a section of
  the song change too quickly?" A jump into a new band belongs at a section boundary or on the
  hook, not in the middle of a verse line.
- **Keep long holds off the register breaks.** Richard Miller puts a mezzo-soprano's passaggi
  around E4 and E5–F5, and a soprano's around E♭4 and F♯5. Pass through them freely, but hold
  long notes on either side. Episode 1's E♭5 peak sits just under a mezzo's upper break.
- **Put open vowels on high notes.** At high pitch, singers retune the first vocal-tract
  resonance to the note, which pushes every vowel towards an open one; Wolfe's lab found Wagner
  gave his sopranos' very highest notes the open-mouthed vowels. /i/ ("see") and /u/ ("who")
  have the lowest first resonance, so they are the first to distort. Episode 1's WHO on E♭5 is
  a risk the scoop and the long "-o-o" help cover; "wha-a-at" is the safer vowel.
- **Lead into a high note.** A step, a scoop, or a breath before it. A high note straight after
  a fast low phrase with no gap is hard for a person to sing. A synthetic voice will sing it,
  but listeners still hear it as strained.
- **Sing it yourself, or render it.** "It is absolutely essential to the craft of songwriting
  that the writer sing the melody, feel it in the voice, reach for the high notes" (Perricone).
  A model can't, so render a guide melody and give it to a human ear.

## What the teachers agree on
- **Mostly steps, with a few leaps for character.** Perricone: "Most vocal melodies consist of
  conjunct motion, which is the most natural and comfortable to sing. It is usually the
  intervallic leaps, however, that give a melody character". Conjunct and disjunct motion
  together "usually produces a good result".
- **Stressed syllables go on high, long notes.** Pattison: stressed syllables are "a) higher in
  pitch b) louder c) longer". A long or high note on an unstressed syllable mispronounces the
  word, so the melody's peaks and holds must land on stressed syllables.
- **A loose verse is fine if the chorus tightens.** Ewer: a meandering verse melody "is fine, as
  long as the chorus melody tightens up, uses a good amount of repetition, and is catchy".
- **Study references in scale degrees.** Hooktheory's TheoryTab database transcribes melodies and
  chords in scale degrees, which makes a reference song's melodic habits comparable with yours.

## Checkable
These can be measured from the score. Run them before any human listens; the human ear then
judges only what the numbers can't. Episode 1's `music/ep01/leaps.mjs` implements the first two.
- **Leap budget.** List every interval of five semitones or more between notes less than a
  beat apart. Outside the chorus there should be almost none, each with a reason.
- **Step ratio per section.** Share of intervals of 0–2 semitones, against the map's targets
  (episode 1: verse and pre-chorus 69–92%, the leapier chorus 57–63%).
- **Range and tessitura.** Report the lowest and highest notes of the song and the
  duration-weighted mean pitch of each section. The chorus mean should be above the verse mean.
- **Climax count and position.** Count onsets on the song's highest pitch, grouping identical
  hook repeats as one. Target: the hook plus one or two more, with the last one held longest.
- **Post-leap reversal.** For each leap of seven or more semitones, check whether the next
  interval turns back. Leaps away from the median that don't turn should be deliberate.
- **Motif similarity.** Encode each line as its rhythm onsets plus its up/down/same contour;
  lines meant to share a motif should match exactly in rhythm and differ in at most one contour
  step. Repeated choruses should match exactly.
- **Strong-beat chord-tone rate.** Share of notes on beats 1 and 3, or held across them, that are
  chord tones. Chorus near 100%; verse lower, but phrase-final notes on 1, 3 or 5.
- **Phrase length and breath gaps.** Split phrases at rests; report each phrase's length in bars
  and the rest before it. Flag phrases over four bars and high notes with no rest before them.
- **Held notes near the breaks.** List notes held over a beat that fall within a semitone of E4
  or E5–F5, for the voice type you use.
- **Vowel on peak notes.** List the vowel of every note in the top four semitones of the range,
  and flag /i/ and /u/ held there.
