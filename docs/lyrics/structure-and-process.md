# Song structure, examples and revision

Detailed guidance for [lyric craft](../../LYRICS.md). Dated expert observations remain
alongside the rules they support; episode examples illustrate their stated scope.
When reading worked examples or sketches from Qing, **take only what an example was given to show**
(a rhyme density, a tone, a structural move) — copying a sketch's exact structure or filling in its
blanks is over-fitting (Qing, 2026-09-27: "there is no particular line structure that works!").

## Structure

### Whole-song shape, hook and payoffs

- **Keep the form lean; every section earns its slot.** Decide the whole-song shape first: find
  the hook, sketch how the song reaches it, and decide what changes after it. Episode 1 used verse,
  pre-chorus, chorus twice, then bridge, breakdown and final chorus; its post-chorus became the
  bridge. That is one worked example, not a checklist to include in full. Stacking extra sections
  (long intros, multiple pre-choruses, breaks, bridges *and* breakdowns) delays the first chorus,
  creates long gaps between hooks, and gives the generator more places to wander. Drop any section
  that doesn't add a distinct turn, contrast or payoff (episode 3 drops the intro, pre-chorus,
  breakdown and outro; episode 4 drops the pre-chorus).
- **Give the singalong phrase a prominent recurring role.** In episode 1, "Good for who? Good for
  what?" became the chorus, while the complaint became its pre-chorus. A band stop helped that
  pop-punk entrance; a patter song can put its memorable line in a recurring verse ending.
- **Within one chorus, the repeat can add.** A chorus sung twice through can use its second pass
  for new ideas rather than restating the first. Match changed repeats to the performer's
  capabilities (see [performer guidance](performers.md#writing-for-the-performer)).
- **The last chorus can answer the hook.** Episode 1 asks "Good for who? Good for what?" three
  times; the last chorus answers it ("Good for you! Good for that!") and its second half turns
  from questions to exclamations. Keep the same sounds so the singalong still works.
- **Put each idea where it belongs, and hold the payoff phrase for last.** When two sections both
  want a line ("I'm someone too"), keep it where it pays off and don't let it leak into the other.
  In a section built to land a phrase, hold it back for the final line once rather than spending
  it early (episode 1's bridge used "who's it for" in lines 2 and 4; now it only closes the
  bridge, held: "who it's fo-o-or?").

### Verses and examples

- **A later verse can answer the first, or shift the premise.** In Qing's rewrite of episode 2
  (2026-09-28), verse 2 lists an oracle for each of verse 1's four failed apps in the same order,
  until the last line breaks the pattern for the joke: no check would have caught Dave, so "you
  really had to let me know". The lesson lands as a payoff to the setup. The bridge also names the
  term outright ("An oracle is something that can help me figure out what's wrong"). A song that
  teaches a word should say what it means at least once. (This reading of her rewrite is ours, not
  her words.) When a second verse shifts the excuse or angle, check whether a repeated pre-chorus
  should update its opening lines on the same beat grid so it answers verse 2 instead of repeating
  verse 1's premise.
- **Choose examples by what they prove, and land them quickly.** A song is not a summary of every
  case in a source article. Give each example a clear teaching job — prefer the clearest evidence
  for that verse's claim over a louder story that muddies it — and keep each one concise so verses
  return to the hook before attention drops.
- **In a list, one example per line — and check each verse's arc.** A different person, app, or
  part of one app gives the picture more to play with and makes the pattern the point. Episode 1's
  verses gave each line a different app; episode 4's piano film drew each line from one gym app
  ([VIDEO.md](../../VIDEO.md#a-story-the-viewer-can-follow) says when each works). Order each
  verse's examples so it builds rather than peaking on its first item and trailing off.
- **Each example carries its own context.** Examples are heard out of context, so each line has to
  say where it is. "2FA to use your gym log" names the app; "No signal: did it save the set?"
  doesn't say gym, so "the set" means nothing (Qing, 2026-09-26: "the examples have to stay
  examples and because they're out of context they have to make the context clear"). And each
  example has to make sense in the story: the gym log is just for me, so Mum logging a set in it
  was "weird" (Qing, 2026-09-27). Borrow a general example, then refit it to who the app is for.
- **Name concrete constraints, not categories.** "Works on the Tube" teaches more than "offline
  support". Check the concrete version doesn't just restate an earlier line ("loads before you
  blink" is "fast to run" again). The same goes for people: a Clawd playing Dad is easy to draw,
  and a Clawd playing "you" isn't (Qing, 2026-09-27).
- **Keep allusions open.** When a word is there to call up something in the news ("going rogue"
  and the agents that broke out of eval environments), keep the word people use and don't narrow
  it ("running on its own" pins it to one meaning). Let the picture drop the hint.

### Transitions

- **A tag breaks the pattern to wind into the next section.** It doesn't need a full rhyme;
  echoing the verse's vowel is enough ("Did I do it wrong? / Oops, your quota's gone! / Guess I
  didn't ask.").

## Process

- **Keep every draft, like a songwriter's notebook.** Each song has a scratchbook
  (`episodes/NN-scratchbook.md`) that holds every lyric sheet, every option offered, and every
  stray rhyme or fragment that worked, even when it didn't fit. Skim it before a local repair (or
  after choosing a new shape on a fresh rethink), and append rather than overwriting. An idea that
  fails now may work later (Qing, 2026-09-27: "Any serious songwriter has pages and pages of notes
  and scratch lyrics for any given song", and "I'm keeping any random rhyme or lyric fragment that
  seems to work well").
- **Match the drafting mode to the song.** For most groove or narrative songs, one writer drafting
  from a locked grid keeps voice, pacing and callbacks tighter, with independent reviewers checking
  craft and claims afterwards (`write-episode/reviewers.md`). Reach for
  [drafting with parallel drafters](../../.claude/skills/songwriting/references/drafting-with-parallel-drafters.md)
  for dense multi-item patter lists (like episode 3) where independent sections split cleanly from
  a shared grid and ledger.
- **Lyrics before storyboard — and keep picture-only labels out of the lyric.** Work on the lyric
  sheet first, noting only the key frames a line depends on, and rebuild the storyboard only once
  the lyrics lock (Qing, 2026-09-24). In conversation too, lyric options come without pictures,
  shots or timings unless a line can't be understood without one. Days of the week and the app's
  name were storyboard details in episode 3 (Qing, 2026-09-29): leave labels to the picture when
  the lyric already supplies enough context, while remembering the picture cannot rescue a missing
  clue in an example heard out of context.
- **Bring the expert whole options.** Two or three finished lines to choose between, each with
  what it covers and what it costs, and a recommendation. Not open questions.

## Maintaining the guide

**Adding a rule.** When a fix teaches something, write down the general lesson, not the fix.
Agents tend to turn one incident into a narrow rule that misses the point. For example, "end
every chorus line on the hook's rhyme" was written after one chorus, but episode 1's chorus
rhymes nothing with its hook. The real lesson was to rhyme more densely and more inventively.
Before you add a rule, check it against a song that worked, and fold it into an existing rule
where one already covers it.
