# Rhythm, groove and song form

How words sit on the beat, how much to syncopate, how drums and phrases shape each section, and how
the song is laid out in time. Each rule is a starting point, and the numbers come from the studies
named. The worked example is episode 1 of *Software Quality Theory 101*: pop-punk at 180 bpm in
4/4, with "We Didn't Start the Fire"-style patter verses, a pre-chorus whose lines run on, a
soaring chorus and a few syncopated pushes. At 180 bpm an eighth note lasts 167 ms and a bar lasts
1.33 s. Grids count eighths: `1 & 2 & 3 & 4 &`.

## Metre and text-setting

- **Listeners infer the metre, so keep its cues on your strong beats.** In Lerdahl & Jackendoff's
  preference rules, note onsets, stressed syllables, long notes, chord changes and a stable bass all
  pull the strong beat onto themselves, and parallel groups get the same metre. A line whose long
  notes and chord changes sit off the bar line can move the bar line in the listener's head.
- **English forbids two kinds of mismatch outright; the rest is taste.** Dell & Halle, building on
  Halle & Lerdahl's textsetting model, define a mismatch as an unstressed syllable on the tactus
  (the beat you tap) next to a stressed one off it. It is ill-formed inside a word ("bal-LOON"
  sung as BAL-loon), or when the unstressed word comes first ("the BOY" with *the* on the beat).
  "SEE me" with *me* on the beat is allowed.
- **Singers repair mismatches by lengthening.** Palmer & Kelly found sung syllables were longer
  when they carried either a linguistic or a metrical accent. A bent stress on a short note costs
  less than one on a held note.
- **Stress alignment is local.** Word-level stress lines up with the beat; phrase-level stress
  mostly doesn't show in placement (Temperley 1999, via Tan, Lustig & Temperley). Check each word
  against its neighbours, not the line's main stress against beat 1.
- **At patter speed the template wins over a bent stress.** A rhythm the listener already knows
  ("We Didn't Start the Fire", straight eighths) carries a line past a mismatch that would jar
  slowly. Episode 1 keeps "TWO eff AY on YOUR gym LOG" and "TWELVE sub A gents". By Dell & Halle
  both are ill-formed (*your* on beat 3 before *gym*; *-A-* on beat 2 inside *subagents*), and the
  expert still preferred them to sixteenth-note fixes that broke the template. Log each bend as an
  exception, and keep it off held notes and rhyme words.
- **Some recent pop mismatches freely.** Lee, Brown & Müllensiefen (cited by Tan, Lustig &
  Temperley) note frequent stress–metre "mismatches" in very recent pop that anticipation doesn't
  explain. Fine in fast, rap-like delivery; a fault in a hook meant to be sung back.

## Syncopation

- **Rock syncopation is mostly anticipation.** Tan, Lustig & Temperley's corpus supports the idea
  that rock melodies come from "underlying, unsyncopated rhythmic representations", with some
  notes "shifted by one beat to precede their underlying positions". Write each push as "X,
  anticipating beat N". If you can't name the beat, it's a cross-rhythm or a mistake.
- **Push into beats 1 and 3, not 2 and 4.** In the rock corpus, stressed syllables collect on the
  `&` of 4 and the `&` of 2, just before the strong beats, far more than on the `&` of 1 or 3. A
  push also keeps the syllable clear of the chords and crashes that land on the beat, so it's
  heard better.
- **It only counts if nothing lands on the next beat.** The positional definition (Longuet-Higgins
  & Lee; Huron & Ommen): a weak-position note followed by a rest or a hold on the next stronger
  beat. Tan, Lustig & Temperley add that the syllable must be stressed. In "STUR-dy or CHEAP ~ ~",
  CHEAP on the `&` of 2, held through beat 3, counts; a stressed `&` that runs straight into a
  syllable on the beat doesn't.
- **Fast songs syncopate at the eighth.** In the corpus, eighth-level syncopation rises with tempo
  and sixteenth-level falls; many songs above 120 bpm have almost no sixteenth onsets. The authors
  suggest the push works best at about a quarter of a second. At 180 bpm, push by an eighth
  (167 ms) or a quarter (333 ms), and keep sixteenths for patter pickups.
- **The genre needs some.** Every stress on the beat sounds like a nursery rhyme. The expert's
  floor for episode 1: "at a minimum 'some one too' would be syncopated". The tag now sings "SOME
  one . TOO ~ ~ ~", TOO on the `&` of 2 and held.
- **Aim for a moderate amount.** Witek et al. (66 listeners, 50 funk drum breaks) found an
  inverted U: "medium degrees of syncopation elicited the most desire to move and the most
  pleasure". That was drums, not melody, so take the direction, not a number.
- **Push the payoff word.** Singers push to fit speech rhythm, to free a syllable from the band's
  hits, and for variety (Tan, Lustig & Temperley). So push CHEAP, TOO, SET and TEST, not *the*.
- **Contrast syncopation by section.** Keep some sections straight so the pushes elsewhere stand
  out, and record each section's target in the song's map. Episode 1's example, pushes per bar:
  verse 0, pre-chorus 0, chorus about 0.1, bridge 0.25, tag 0.5.

## Groove, microtiming and drums

- **Groove is wanting to move, and it lives in the pattern.** Listeners' own definitions converge
  on "a pleasurable urge to move" (Janata, Tomic & Haberman). Frühauf, Kopiez & Platz (93 music
  students; kick and snare shifted ±15 and ±25 ms) found quantised versions rated highest. Davies
  et al. found quantised and as-played rated equally, with groove falling as deviations grew. Get
  the grid pattern right first and keep humanisation small.
- **Punk feel is the pattern plus the pushes.** Drumeo: "snare drum backbeats on every upbeat (or
  the 'and's), quick doubles played on the bass drum, and 'sloshy' open hi-hats". Their 180 bpm
  example is New Found Glory's "My Friends Over You". When the singer pushes, the band pushes with
  them (guitars, bass and crash on the `&` of 4) or holds; never half the band.
- **Section patterns** (beyond MUSIC.md's defaults):
  - Verse: closed-hat eighths, backbeat, a sparse kick, so the patter's consonants cut through.
  - Pre-chorus: shorten the repeating unit. In Summach's "Talking in Your Sleep" the kick goes
    "from a four-beat repeating pattern to a two-beat pattern". Floor-tom eighths or kick quarters
    do the same.
  - Chorus: open hats or crash-ride, or the skank beat (kick on the beat, snare on every `&`; "fast
    and heavy polkas", drumlessons.com) for the biggest lift.
  - Double-time doubles the snare rate at the same tempo, for a last chorus or outro. Half-time
    puts the snare on 3 only, for a bridge or breakdown; a chant sits on kick 1 and snare 3.
  - Fills: one or two beats. A full-bar sixteenth fill at 180 bpm blurs. End before the downbeat
    and crash with the kick on the one.

## Phrase structure and hypermetre

- **Hypermetre is metre one level up.** Lerdahl & Jackendoff prefer "every other beat" strong at
  each level. Listeners hear a strong bar at a chord change, at the start of a group ("strong beat
  early"), and on the first of two repeated patterns (Temperley 2008). A chorus that starts with a
  new chord and riff is a strong bar; one that starts mid-phrase isn't.
- **A pickup on counts 6 7 8 is the classic way in.** That's beats 3, 4 and the `&` of 4 of the bar
  before. Episode 1 speaks "guess I didn't ask" there, a cappella after the band cuts on GONE.
  Count it in the bar before; it doesn't lengthen the next section.
- **Give an exclamation space.** "Oops!" needs rests around it and the band stopping or hitting
  once with it. Episode 1 gives OOPS its own downbeat and a rest, after a silent bar that follows
  "did I do it WRONG?".
- **Elision: one bar ends a phrase and starts the next.** Summach, after Stephenson, calls it
  "paradigmatic for much pop/rock music": a unit's final chord is "asserted as the (premature)
  onset of a new four-bar grouping". Count the bar once, in the new phrase, and note it in the map.
- **Run lines on to build urgency.** Lines that end on the next line's downbeat ("…WHO it's | FOR
  you DIDn't TELL me WHAT they | WANT") remove the verse's rests. In Summach's example the
  lyric–rest alternation "quickens to single bars", then the last line "drives through two bars,
  omitting the rest altogether".
- **An irregular length is an extension.** Summach's "Sugar Shack" has four-bar phrases except the
  departure, "extended to six bars by the addition of a third two-bar fragment". A six-bar
  pre-chorus reads as 4 + 2, a phrase that went on past its expected end, so the extra bars must
  build (new chord, rising line, fill, stop). The expert compared 5, 6 and 8 bars by ear and kept 6.
- **Spend the listener's expectation once per boundary.** After eight regular bars they predict the
  next downbeat. One bar short is a jolt; bars added are suspense. Two irregularities in a row sound
  like miscounting. Temperley: hypermetrical shifts "help to emphasize the formal division" between
  sections, and frequent ones impart "heightened tension".
- **Write pickup bars as pickup bars.** Episode 1's chorus opens on a crash bar with "GOOD for" on
  beat 3, so WHO lands on its second bar. Map it as 1 + 16, not 17.

## Song form

- **Verse–pre-chorus–chorus is a sentence stretched out.** Open Music Theory, after Summach: verse
  is statement and restatement; the pre-chorus is the departure ("fragmentation, acceleration of
  harmonic rhythm, and movement away from tonic harmony"), "most significantly typified in energy
  gain"; the chorus concludes, usually on the tonic, with "a more dense or active instrumental
  texture; prominent background vocals; and/or a higher register melody".
- **A post-chorus keeps the energy and adds a hook.** Asaf Peres's test (via Wikipedia): it
  "maintains or increases sonic energy" and "contains a melodic hook"; otherwise it's a bridge or a
  transition. Episode 1's gang echoes, "(who)-o" and "(what)-at", are a small attached one.
- **The bridge withholds the chorus.** Its job is "generating high expectation for the return of
  the primary module", often ending on the dominant (Open Music Theory). A pop-punk breakdown does
  the same job stripped down: half-time, a chant, then a rebuild.
- **End on material already heard.** "Rock songs almost always end with material that has been
  heard earlier" (Summach, via Open Music Theory). Episode 1 ends on the intro's stab so it loops.
- **Energy rises in steps.** Each chorus a little bigger than the last; the bridge or breakdown is
  the dip before the final climb; the final pre-chorus can start quiet so its build is audible.
- **Get to the voice and the hook fast.** Léveillé Gauvin (303 top-10 singles, 1986–2015): intros
  fell from over 20 s in the mid-1980s to about 5 s, "a 78 percent drop"; once the singing started
  the title arrived about 18% sooner; tempo rose about 8%. Klimmt et al. extend the series to
  2020. Hit Songs Deconstructed measured 39 s to the first chorus in 2017 top-10 pop, 30 s in hip
  hop. Chartmetric: the average Spotify charting song in 2024 ran "around 3 minutes", 30 s shorter
  than in 2019.
- **Short-form video wants the idea in the first clip.** Berklee's songwriting chair: "tell us what
  it's going to be in 30 seconds", "super catchy, really fast", but "be very selective about what
  you introduce first". The same article says the bridge has "largely vanished" from TikTok-era
  hits. For a muted autoplay feed: the hook's rhythm in the first three seconds (instrument or
  caption), the sung hook inside 30 seconds, and a last bar that loops into the first. Episode 1:
  voice in the first second, guitar hook at 0:03, sung hook at about 0:25, 2:26 in all.

## Contrast between sections

- **Contrast density.** Episode 1's verse lines put seven syllables in a bar and its chorus hook
  about three. A chorus as busy as its verse won't feel like an arrival, however loud the band.
- **Contrast note length.** Patter in eighths, one held word per pre-chorus line, a hook held for a
  bar or more. The step up in length makes the chorus soar as much as the step up in pitch.
- **Contrast syncopation.** Square verse and pre-chorus, pushed chorus tail and tag. The pushes only
  register because their neighbours are square.
- **Place silence exactly.** A full-band stop before the chorus, four silent counts after a question,
  a rest beside an exclamation. The listener keeps counting through silence, so it must last a
  whole number of beats and the next event must land where they predicted.
- **A stop must read as a stop.** A hard cut (a hit, then nothing) of at least two beats at
  180 bpm, then the crash, kick, chord and hook word together on the downbeat after.

## Checkable

From the score, with lead events as (bar, eighth, syllable, duration) and drum events per section.
Take stress from a pronouncing dictionary plus a function-word list, not the score's capitals:
those mark where the writer *put* stress, so checking against them is circular.

| Rule | Method |
|---|---|
| Stress-on-beat rate | Per section, the share of stressed syllables on quarter beats. Hook over 90%; patter lower, each bend logged. |
| Illicit mismatches | Flag Dell & Halle cases (word-internal, or unstressed word on the tactus before a stressed one off it). Error on held notes and rhyme words, warning elsewhere. |
| Syncopation index | Per section, stressed syllables on an `&` with a rest or hold on the next beat, per bar, against the map's targets. Sections should contrast, and no section pushes on most bars. |
| Anticipation target | Each push sits on the `&` of 4 or of 2; list any on the `&` of 1 or 3. |
| Pushes fit the next chord | Each push's pitch is a tone of the chord on the beat it anticipates. |
| Rhythm density | Lead onsets per bar per section, against the map's targets; sections should contrast (episode 1's chorus about 3 against the verse's 7). |
| Note length | Mean lead duration per section in eighths, against the map's targets (episode 1: chorus > pre-chorus > verse). |
| Phrase lengths | Bars per section and per line (lines split at rests of an eighth or more). Flag anything not 2, 4, 8 or 16 without a reason in the map. |
| Parallel lines share rhythm | Jaccard overlap of onset positions between lines with the same motif ID: at least 0.8; chorus repeats exact. |
| Time to first hook | Seconds to the hook's first sung onset and first instrumental statement, against the map's targets (episode 1: 0:25 and 0:03). |
| Section lengths | Bars × 240 / bpm per section; report the intro, the time to first chorus and the total. |
| Silence placement | Every lead rest of two beats or more and every band stop, with what follows. Each stop ends in a downbeat hit; each exclamation has a rest beside it. |
| Drum contrast | Cymbal onsets per bar and snare positions per section; chorus above verse, half-time snare on 3 only. |
| Drum microtiming | Standard deviation of drum onsets from the grid stays within the score header's budget of a few ms. |
